    0 IMPELLER BLADE DESIGN METHOD

    What each formula does to the blade

    In 1974, Willem Jansen and Andreas Kirschner at Northern Research & Engineering published a way to design centrifugal‑compressor impeller blades not by drawing a shape, but by prescribing the velocity the flow should have along the blade — and calculating the geometry that produces it.     1 INITIAL VELOCITY ESTIMATE

    The passage shape sets the speed

    Before any blade exists, the paper estimates how fast the flow must travel through the bare hub‑to‑shroud passage. Mass has to get through a shrinking or growing annulus, so velocity is fixed by geometry through continuity:

    EQ. 1 — CONTINUITY AT A NORMAL STATION
    ṁ = ρ · Cm · 2π r · b
    ṁ mass flow · ρ density · Cm meridional velocity · r radius · b passage width (hub‑to‑shroud normal distance)

    Drag the hub/shroud radii or mass flow above — narrower passages or higher flow force C_m up; that's why velocity spikes near the discharge, where the annulus area shrinks fastest.

    2 HUB / SHROUD SPLIT

    One average speed, three streamlines

    Eq. 1 only gives the passage-average velocity. To load the hub, shroud, and an intermediate streamline individually, the method needs each of their speeds separately. Two assumptions get there: zero circulation across a normal, and a linear variation of flow between hub and shroud.

    EQ. 2 — ZERO CIRCULATION ACROSS A NORMAL
    Cm,h · dmh  =  Cm,s · dms
    EQ. 3 — LINEAR VARIATION BETWEEN HUB AND SHROUD
    Cm,mean·rmean  =  ½ ( Cm,h·rh + Cm,s·rs )
    Solved together (the paper's eqs. 4–5) these split the one mean value from §1 into a hub curve and a shroud curve.

    Notice the shroud streamline always runs faster than the hub — it's doing more of the turning, covers more distance per station, and (as §5 will show) ends up carrying almost all of the blade loading.

    3 PRESCRIBED LOADING

    You choose how hard the blade works, station by station

    This is the method's namesake move. Instead of guessing a shape, the designer prescribes Wp − Ws — the velocity difference between the blade's pressure and suction faces — at every point along the camber line. Integrating that loading builds up the angular momentum the flow has picked up so far:

    EQ. 6 — LOADING → ANGULAR MOMENTUM
    r Cθ(s)  =  Z2π ∫0s (Wp − Ws) ds′
    Z number of blades · s camber‑line distance. The last 10% of chord is forced toward zero loading to satisfy the Kutta condition at the trailing edge.

    4 FLOW ANGLE

    Angular momentum becomes a direction

    At each station the meridional velocity (§1) and the swirl velocity implied by the loading (§3) combine, in the rotating frame, into the relative flow angle β — the angle the blade metal has to point.

    EQ. 7 — RELATIVE FLOW ANGLE
    β  =  tan−1 Cθ − rωCm
    ω angular velocity (rad/s) · rω is the blade's own tangential speed U at that radius.
    VELOCITY TRIANGLE
    U (blade speed) C (absolute) W (relative), ∠β

    Scrub through the blade: near the leading edge, swirl is still zero, so β is steep — almost tangential, set entirely by blade speed. By the trailing edge the prescribed loading has built up enough swirl that β eases toward the discharge blade angle.

    5 INTEGRATING TO A SHAPE

    Stack up the angles, get a spiral

    β is a direction at one station. To get the blade's actual angular position, the method walks station to station and accumulates it:

    EQ. 8 — BLADE ANGLE BY INTEGRATION OF β
    θ(s)  =  ∫0s tan βr ds′
    θ is initialized to zero at the leading edge here (the paper anchors it at the trailing edge on the shroud; the choice is just a rotational reference and doesn't change the shape).

    This is the step that actually draws the blade: a running sum of small turns. Looking straight down the shaft, the camber line traces the spiral below — the same curve visible edge‑on in the 3D model above.

    6 STRAIGHT‑LINE ELEMENTS

    Three curves, one straight line — or not

    Independently loading the hub, mean, and shroud streamlines produces three different space curves. A castable, millable blade needs its surface built from straight lines connecting them (eq. 9a–9b) — which only works cleanly if all three curves reach the trailing edge at close to the same angle. If they don't, the blade twists — "excessive lean," in the paper's words.

    EQ. 9A / 9B — RULED-SURFACE ELEMENT CONSTRUCTION
    Δs2 / Δs1  =  t̂ · (t̂₁ × l̂)t̂₁ · (t̂₂ × l̂)
    Each new line element's intersections with the other two curves are found from the local tangents t̂ and the previous element's direction l̂ — building the surface one straight rung at a time.

    The paper's own impeller ran into this: an early design leaned badly enough at the inducer to worry about root bending stress. Its fix — the one used here by default — is to drop independent hub/mid loading and instead specify blade‑element orientation directly, keeping every rung perfectly radial (zero lean) while still following the shroud's prescribed loading.     7 THICKNESS & ASSEMBLY

    From camber line to metal

    Everything so far describes the camber surface — an infinitely thin sheet. The last step offsets it, along its local surface normal, by half the prescribed thickness in each direction to get pressure and suction faces — tapered thinner near the trailing edge, per the Kutta condition, and blended to a rounded nose at the leading edge. Set the taper up in the hero controls; the two side surfaces are colour‑coded brass (pressure) and cyan (suction) throughout this page and in the 3D model, matching the loading plots in §3.

    This is also where the paper's own caveats live: the generated thickness is strictly trapezoidal (constant taper along every straight‑line element) — an exponential taper for stress relief has to be added afterward by hand, and it doesn't change the aerodynamics much if kept modest.

    Scroll back up — every slider on this page feeds the same 3D model. That's the whole method in one loop: prescribe loading → get a flow angle → integrate an angle → connect straight lines → add thickness → blade.

    i SIMPLIFICATIONS IN THIS TOOL

    This page implements the paper's design logic end‑to‑end but takes shortcuts the original computer program did not, in order to stay interactively explorable:

    • Density is estimated from inlet/exit stagnation temperature only (no full compressible-flow iteration); the paper reconverges density, streamline position, and loading over several passes.
    • The angular‑momentum/loading relation (eq. 6) is implemented in a simplified circulation form; the paper's actual derivation and the intermediate blade‑to‑blade analysis aren't reproduced.
    • Camber‑line distance is approximated as meridional arc length (the paper's own stated initial estimate) rather than iterated to convergence.
    • The "radial element" blade uses the shroud streamline's loading only, matching the paper's own practical revision — hub/mid velocity distributions shown are the resulting outputs, not independently controlled.
    • No detailed flow‑field reanalysis, slip correction, or inlet‑incidence leading‑edge treatment is performed.
